Why Marawi Was Different

By Dr. Paul K. Mears · September 30, 2026 · 7 min read

On May 23, 2017, a botched raid to capture Isnilon Hapilon turned into five months of urban combat. Marawi was not the first time militants held ground in Mindanao. It was the first time they tried to hold a city.

For most of its history the Abu Sayyaf operated like a guerrilla force and, increasingly, like a criminal enterprise: small cells, jungle camps, kidnappings for ransom. Marawi broke that pattern. A coalition of Hapilon's faction, the Maute brothers, and foreign fighters declared a wilayah and dug in among civilian buildings, tunnels, and mosques.

A coalition, not a cell

The siege worked, for a time, because it fused local clan networks with the brand and resources of the Islamic State. That fusion is the through-line of The Mindanao Shadow: local grievance supplying the manpower, transnational ideology supplying the purpose.

Insurgencies rarely change their tactics overnight. They change their sponsors, and the tactics follow.

Lessons still being learned

For security professionals, Marawi is a case study in how quickly an organization that looks like a criminal gang can reorganize into a conventional fighting force when given a flag to rally behind. It is also a lesson in the cost of reconstruction, a cost measured in years, not months.

I cover the siege, the government's response, and the long road to the Bangsamoro in the final chapters of the book.
